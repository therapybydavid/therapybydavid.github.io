Moderate Depression

Your score falls in the moderate range. Many people at this level feel like they're functioning on the outside while struggling underneath. Therapy at this level tends to make a meaningful difference.

What this can look like day to day

This can look like dragging yourself through the day, going through the motions at work or at home while feeling disconnected from it all. You might pull back from people you care about, lose interest in things that used to matter, or find yourself thinking nothing is going to get better — even when life looks okay from the outside.

Moderate depression is one of the most common presentations in outpatient therapy. It responds well to structured, consistent work — particularly approaches that identify the underlying patterns keeping you stuck rather than just treating the symptoms on the surface.

Moderate Anxiety

Your score falls in the moderate range for anxiety. Worry may feel hard to control, restlessness or irritability may show up more than you'd like, and your nervous system may feel chronically activated.

What this can look like day to day

This often looks like a nervous system that never fully turns off. You might feel on edge in conversations, irritable with people you care about, or exhausted from the mental effort of keeping everything together. Sleep can be disrupted, concentration can slip, and the worry tends to jump from one thing to the next without ever fully resolving.

At the moderate level, anxiety is often being managed — through staying busy, avoiding certain situations, or simply pushing through. Those coping strategies cost energy, and they rarely address the source. Therapy creates a different path: understanding where the pattern comes from and building a steadier response.

What Your Results Together Mean

Depression and anxiety rarely travel alone.

Your results show moderate scores in both areas — which is more common than most people realize. Research consistently shows that depression and anxiety occur together in the majority of people who experience either one. They feed each other: anxiety keeps the nervous system activated and braced for threat, while depression drains the energy needed to respond. The result can feel like being simultaneously wound up and shut down.

Why they overlap

Both conditions share underlying patterns — negative thought loops, avoidance, nervous system dysregulation. Addressing one often reduces the other.

What this means for you

Having both doesn't mean things are worse — it means there's a clear target. Therapy that addresses the root patterns tends to move both scores at once.

The important thing to know: moderate scores in both depression and anxiety are very treatable. This is not a permanent state. These patterns developed for reasons — and they can change with the right kind of consistent support.

The Work

What therapy actually addresses at this level.

At moderate scores in both depression and anxiety, therapy is not about venting — it is about identifying and shifting the specific patterns that keep you stuck. Here is what that typically looks like in practice:

1
Naming the pattern

What keeps repeating? Where does it show up — at work, in relationships, in how you talk to yourself? Getting specific about the pattern is the first step toward changing it.

2
Understanding what drives it

Anxiety and depression at this level usually make sense once you understand the experiences and beliefs underneath them. The goal is not to pathologize the past, but to understand how it still shapes the present.

3
Building practical tools

Skills for regulating the nervous system, shifting thought patterns, improving communication, and setting limits that actually hold — not in theory, but in real situations you face each week.

4
Connecting it to real life

Every session connects to something outside the room. The work only matters if it changes how you show up in actual decisions, conversations, and relationships.
