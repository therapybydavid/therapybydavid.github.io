## The Question People Don't Ask Out Loud Most people don't call and ask, "What happens to the things I tell you?" They ask about scheduling, or cost, or whether David takes their insurance. The confidentiality question usually stays unspoken, but it shapes the whole first few sessions anyway. A new client, say a mid-level manager at an energy company or a Houston police sergeant, will often test the waters before saying anything real. They'll talk in generalities. They'll leave names out. They'll watch to see what the therapist does with a hard admission before deciding whether to bring the next one. This is a completely reasonable strategy, and it's also the main reason therapy can feel slow or pointless in the beginning: caution and honesty don't usually fit in the same sentence. The fix isn't willpower. It's information. Once someone actually understands what's protected, what isn't, and where the lines are, the guardedness tends to ease on its own. If you're still deciding whether to start at all, [What to Expect in Your First Therapy Session](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/first-therapy-session) covers the practical side of walking in the door. This post covers what happens after you're in the room. ## What Confidentiality Actually Means in Practice Licensed therapists in Texas operate under both state law and HIPAA, and the baseline rule is simple: nothing said in session can be shared with anyone, including a spouse, a parent, an employer, or another provider, without the client's written, informed consent. Not a phone call to "check in." Not a casual mention if the two of you happen to know the same people. This surprises people more than you'd expect. A common scenario: someone starts individual therapy while also seeing a couples therapist, or their partner is curious about what came up in a session together. The assumption is often that therapists compare notes freely or that information flows sideways. It doesn't, not without a signed release specifying exactly what can be shared, with whom, and for how long. Confidentiality isn't a professional courtesy. It's a legal and ethical requirement, and it exists specifically so that the therapy room can be one of the few places where a person doesn't have to manage how they're perceived. ## The Exceptions Almost Nobody Explains Well Confidentiality has limits, and vague awareness of them ("they have to report certain things") tends to make people more anxious, not less, because they don't know exactly where the line is. The real exceptions are narrow: a credible, imminent risk of serious harm to the client or someone else, suspected abuse or neglect of a child, elderly person, or dependent adult, and rare situations involving a court order. That's close to the whole list. Here's where the confusion usually comes from. A client says, in the middle of a hard week, "I just don't want to be here anymore, I'm so tired of all of it." That sentence, on its own, is not the same as a plan. A skilled therapist knows the difference between exhausted language and an actual safety concern, and most of the time the response is more conversation, not a phone call to anyone. The exceptions exist for genuine emergencies, not for every strong feeling that gets said out loud. If this distinction is something you're actively worried about, it's a fair and useful thing to ask a therapist directly in a consultation call, before you've said anything you're nervous about. ## The Paper Trail: What's Actually in Your File Therapists keep two different kinds of records, and they're treated very differently under the law. The first is the progress note, a brief, factual entry required after each session: date, general topics covered, clinical observations, any safety concerns, treatment plan updates. This becomes part of your official record and could, in specific legal circumstances, be requested. The second is the psychotherapy process note, the therapist's own working notes: impressions, hypotheses, the small details that help them track patterns session to session. These carry extra protection under HIPAA specifically because they're not meant to be a transcript, they're a clinical tool. They're stored separately, they're rarely if ever released even under subpoena, and no one, including insurance companies, has an automatic right to see them. A client once asked, half joking, "Are you writing down everything I say?" The honest answer is no. What gets recorded is thematic: what came up, what shifted, what to follow up on, not a word-for-word account of a session. Knowing that distinction alone tends to loosen people up considerably. ## How Insurance Changes the Picture This is the part that rarely gets explained clearly, and it matters more than most people realize. When you use insurance for therapy, a diagnosis is required for reimbursement. That diagnosis becomes part of a medical record that can, in some cases, be accessed during underwriting for life insurance, disability insurance, or certain background checks, and it can follow you in ways that are hard to undo later. For a veteran or a first responder concerned about a security clearance, or an executive wary of anything showing up in a future review, this isn't a small consideration. It's often the actual reason someone chooses to pay privately rather than run therapy through insurance at all: no diagnosis code required for a third party, no claims history, no external record beyond what you and your therapist keep. This is also part of why the format of therapy itself, in person versus by video, is worth thinking through deliberately. [Online Therapy vs. In-Person: Which Is Right for You?](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/online-vs-in-person-therapy) walks through some of those tradeoffs, including privacy. Private pay isn't realistic for everyone's budget, and that's a legitimate concern. Most established practices, including this one, keep a limited number of reduced-fee or sliding-scale spots, and many therapists can point clients toward nonprofit therapy networks built specifically around income-based pricing if a full private-pay rate isn't workable right now. ## Questions Worth Asking Before Your First Session A short list, worth asking any therapist you're considering, not just David: What exactly goes into my file, and who could ever access it. What would actually have to happen for you to break confidentiality. Do you ever consult with colleagues about my case, and if so, how is that handled. What happens to my records if I stop coming, or if you retire or close your practice. A client who asked these questions directly, before their first real session, described it afterward as the thing that let her actually talk instead of managing what she said. That's usually the effect. Most licensed therapists expect these questions and welcome them, because a client who trusts the structure of the room tends to do better work inside it. ## Privacy Isn't the Same as Secrecy It's worth naming the difference, because they get confused. Privacy is structural: legal protections, careful recordkeeping, a professional obligation to keep your information contained. Secrecy is personal: avoiding a truth even inside the one space built for you to say it out loud. The goal of confidentiality isn't to give you a place to hide things from the people in your life forever. It's to give you one space with fewer consequences for honesty, so you can actually look at something clearly before deciding what, if anything, to do with it outside that room. That's a meaningfully different kind of privacy than most people expect walking in. If you've been holding back in therapy, or holding off on starting at all, because you weren't sure where your words would go, that's a reasonable thing to bring up in a first conversation.
