Most couples don't wake up one day and announce, "our values no longer align." It happens quietly. One of you wants to spend a Saturday at church and the other wants to sleep in. One of you saves every extra dollar and the other believes money is for living now. One of you wants to raise kids with strict routines and the other grew up without any and turned out fine, thanks.

None of these moments feel like a crisis on their own. But they stack. And at some point, a lot of couples end up asking the same question in my office: is this a real difference in who we are, or just a disagreement we haven't worked through yet?

The answer usually depends on what kind of difference it actually is.

## Values, Preferences, and the Difference Between Them

It helps to separate three things that often get lumped together as "our values don't match":

**Core values** are the things that shape how you see right and wrong, how you want to treat people, and what kind of life feels honest to live. Honesty, fidelity, how you want to raise children, whether you want children at all, how you treat family, what role faith plays in your life — these tend to sit close to identity.

**Preferences** are the things you have opinions about but could genuinely live differently without feeling like you'd betrayed yourself. How you spend a Saturday. Whether you keep a tidy house. How much you socialize versus stay in.

**Habits shaped by stress or circumstance** get mistaken for values all the time. Someone who's anxious about money isn't necessarily "materialistic" — they might be carrying fear from how money was handled growing up. Someone who avoids conflict isn't necessarily "non-confrontational by nature" — they might have learned early that conflict wasn't safe.

 The mistake a lot of couples make is treating every difference as if it belongs in the first category. That's how a disagreement about how often to visit in-laws turns into "we want fundamentally different lives." Sometimes that's true. Often it isn't.

## Three Questions Worth Sitting With

Before deciding a value gap is unworkable, it's worth getting specific:

**Does this difference show up in how you treat each other, or just in what you each prefer?** A partner who lies to avoid conflict is showing you something about honesty. A partner who wants a quieter social life than you do is showing you a preference. Those aren't the same category of problem, even if they both create friction.

**Has this always been true, or is it new?** People do genuinely change — through therapy, faith, parenthood, loss, or just getting older. The question isn't whether change happened. It's whether there's still room for both of you inside the relationship as you are now, or whether one of you has quietly stopped fitting.

**Can you each still respect how the other lives, even where you differ?** You don't have to share every value to stay together. But respect tends to be non-negotiable. If one of you privately views the other's beliefs, parenting style, or way of living as something to fix or wait out, that's a harder problem than a simple difference of opinion.

## When "We're Just Different People Now" Means Something Else

Sometimes couples use the language of values because it feels cleaner than the alternative. It's easier to say "we grew apart" than to say "I've stopped trusting you," or "I don't feel chosen anymore," or "I'm scared of what it means if I admit this isn't working." If you notice the values conversation keeps circling without landing anywhere, it's worth asking what's actually underneath it. This is often where [couples get stuck having the same argument](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/couples-same-argument) without realizing the real disagreement is somewhere else entirely.

And sometimes the values really have diverged — not because either of you did anything wrong, but because you're both different people than you were when the relationship started. That doesn't make the relationship a failure. It does mean the conversation deserves more care than either silence or an ultimatum. If you and your partner still care about each other but keep bumping into different visions of the future, that's its own kind of hard, and it's covered in more depth in [When You Still Love Each Other But Want Different Things](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/couples-same-argument).

## Working Through It, Not Around It

Sorting real values from preferences and old habits isn't something most couples can do cleanly in the middle of an argument. It usually takes some distance, and often a third person in the room who isn't invested in either of you being right.
