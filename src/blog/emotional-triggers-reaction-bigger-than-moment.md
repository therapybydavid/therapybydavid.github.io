## The Comment Was Small. The Reaction Wasn't.

A coworker mentions, in passing, that your report "could have been a little more thorough." You say something neutral, walk back to your desk, and spend the next three hours replaying it. By the time you get home, you're short with your partner over nothing related. Or: your partner is fifteen minutes late without texting, and by the time they walk in, you're not annoyed, you're furious, and some part of you is already rehearsing the breakup speech. In both cases, the size of the reaction doesn't match the size of the event. That mismatch is the tell. It's usually the clearest sign that something old got activated by something new, and that you're not actually reacting to the report or the fifteen minutes. You're reacting to what the report or the lateness reminded you of.

## Triggers Are Time Travel, Not Overreaction

It's tempting to label these moments as overreacting, and then to feel embarrassed or ashamed about it afterward. But that framing skips the more useful question: overreacting compared to what? If the reaction feels like a 9 and the event was a 2, the other 7 points came from somewhere. They're not manufactured out of nothing. They're usually on loan from an earlier experience where a similar feeling (being dismissed, being made to wait, being criticized in front of others) actually was a 9, because of what it meant at the time or what came after it. A client once described it well: "It's like the current moment opens a door, and something from ten years ago walks through it before I can stop it." That's a fair description of how triggers work. The nervous system isn't reading the situation in front of you as much as it's pattern-matching against situations it already knows how to survive. For more on why the body responds this way before the thinking brain catches up, see [Your Anxiety Isn't Just in Your Head, It's in Your Body](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/anxiety-in-the-body-not-just-the-mind).

## What People Try First (and Why It Doesn't Hold)

Most people handle a trigger reaction in one of three ways, and all three have limits. The first is apologizing for the size of the reaction without understanding it. "Sorry, I don't know why I got so upset." This can smooth things over in the moment, but it treats the reaction as an inconvenience to manage rather than information to use. Nothing changes, so the same thing happens again next month. The second is arguing about the surface content. If the trigger was the fifteen minutes late, the fight becomes about punctuality, traffic, whose fault it was. Both people can be technically right about the facts and still miss what the fight was actually about, which is why these conversations often end without resolution even when everyone apologizes. This is part of why [couples keep having the same argument](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/couples-same-argument) even when the topic changes every time. The third is suppression: swallowing the reaction, deciding it isn't worth mentioning, and moving on. This one looks the most mature from the outside, but the charge doesn't disappear, it just gets stored. It tends to resurface later, often at a worse moment, aimed at someone who had nothing to do with the original wound.

## The Three Questions That Reveal a Trigger

When a reaction feels bigger than the moment warrants, three questions can help locate what's actually happening, ideally asked after the initial heat has dropped a little. First: what did this remind me of? Not what it's about on the surface, but what it felt like. Being fifteen minutes late might not be the issue. Feeling like an afterthought might be. Second: is there an earlier version of this feeling? A parent who was chronically unavailable, a partner who used lateness as a form of control, a childhood where being forgotten meant something specific. The current event doesn't need to resemble the old one exactly. It just needs to produce the same emotional signature. Third: what did I need back then that I didn't get? This is the question that usually points toward what would actually help now, whether that's reassurance, an apology, space, or simply having the reaction taken seriously instead of minimized. A concrete example: a client kept getting disproportionately angry when her husband checked his phone mid-conversation. The surface issue was phone etiquette. The real issue, once she slowed down enough to ask these questions, was growing up with a parent who was physically present but emotionally checked out, and the specific, familiar sting of trying to talk to someone who wasn't really there. Once she named that out loud to her husband, the conversation changed from a fight about phones to a conversation about attention, which was the actual thing at stake.

## Naming a Trigger Doesn't Automatically Loosen Its Grip

It's worth saying plainly: understanding where a reaction comes from doesn't switch it off. Plenty of people can explain their trigger in detail and still get hijacked by it the next time it shows up. That's not a failure of insight, it's just how these patterns are built. They live in the body and in fast, automatic circuits, not only in the part of the brain doing the explaining. If you've ever thought "I know exactly why I do this, so why can't I stop," you're describing something [covered in more depth here](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/insight-trap), and it's one of the more common frustrations that brings people into therapy in the first place. What actually loosens a trigger's grip over time is closer to practice than insight: noticing it earlier in the sequence, tolerating the discomfort of the old feeling without immediately acting on it, and slowly giving the nervous system new evidence that the current moment is not the old one. That takes repetition, and it usually takes it in relationship with someone else, whether that's a partner learning to respond differently or a therapist helping you catch the pattern in real time.

## What to Try in the Moment

When you notice a reaction that feels too big for what's happening, a few small moves can create enough space to work with it instead of being run by it. Name the size gap out loud, even just to yourself: "this feels like a lot for what actually happened." That sentence alone often takes some of the charge out of it. Delay the response, not to suppress it, but to let the initial spike pass. A few minutes rarely changes the facts of a situation, but it changes how much of the old feeling is driving the response. Track the pattern over a week or two rather than trying to solve it in a single moment. Triggers tend to repeat around specific themes (feeling dismissed, feeling controlled, feeling forgotten). Once you can name the theme, you can often catch it faster the next time it shows up, similar to how [catching an anxiety spiral early](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/anxiety-spiral-catch-it-before-it-snowballs) is easier than trying to interrupt it once it's already snowballed.

## When It's Worth Bringing to Therapy

Some triggers are minor and manageable on your own. Others point to something more persistent, especially if the same reaction keeps showing up across different relationships, jobs, and situations, or if it's strong enough to damage relationships you care about. A therapist can help you trace a trigger back to where it started, work through the original experience if it's still carrying weight, and build new responses that don't require you to white-knuckle your way through every reminder of it. If you've noticed a pattern like this in your own life and want help understanding where it comes from, reach out to schedule a session. Working through it with someone trained to help is often the difference between managing a trigger and actually changing your relationship to it.
