## The Pattern Has a Name

One partner brings up a concern. The other goes quiet, changes the subject, or leaves the room. The first partner, feeling unheard, brings it up again, this time with more urgency. The second partner retreats further. Within a few minutes, one person is trying harder to connect and the other is trying harder to disengage, and neither of them planned for it to go this way. This is sometimes called the pursuer-distancer cycle, and it shows up in a large number of the couples I see, regardless of how long they have been together or how much they genuinely love each other. It is not about the topic of the argument. It is about what happens to the conversation itself once one person leans in and the other leans away.

## What It Looks Like at Home

A husband notices his wife has been quiet for two days after a disagreement about money. He wants to fix it, so he brings it up again over dinner. She says she needs to think, and gets up to do the dishes. He follows her into the kitchen to keep talking. She goes quiet and starts scrubbing a pot that is already clean. He raises his voice slightly, not out of anger but out of frustration at being shut out. She says, calmly, "I can't do this right now," and walks upstairs. From the outside, it looks like he is the one causing the tension by pushing. From his side, it looks like she is the one causing it by refusing to engage. Both are describing the same fifteen minutes accurately. Neither is lying. They are just standing on opposite ends of the same cycle, and each one's behavior is triggering more of the other's.

## Why "Just Talk About It" Doesn't Work

Most couples try to solve this by talking about the underlying issue harder. The pursuing partner reads a book on communication and comes back with better phrasing. The distancing partner agrees to "really try" next time. And then the next disagreement happens, adrenaline shows up, and both people revert to the exact same roles, because the roles were never really about vocabulary. The reason this approach falls short is that it treats the cycle as a content problem, something that better words or more patience can fix in the moment. But by the time someone is pursuing or withdrawing, they are usually already flooded. Words chosen in that state rarely reflect what either person actually wants to say. This is part of why couples can have the [same argument](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/couples-same-argument) for years without it ever resolving. The topic keeps changing. The pattern does not.

## The Nervous System Underneath the Behavior

Pursuing and distancing are not just communication styles. They are nervous system responses. The pursuing partner's body often reads silence or distance as a threat to the relationship, and pursuing is an attempt to re-establish safety by closing the gap. The distancing partner's body often reads conflict itself as a threat, and withdrawing is an attempt to re-establish safety by reducing stimulation. Both people are trying to calm down. They are just using opposite strategies to do it, and each strategy makes the other person's nervous system more activated, not less. The pursuer's chasing feels like pressure to the distancer, so the distancer shuts down further. The distancer's silence feels like abandonment to the pursuer, so the pursuer pushes harder. This is the same feedback loop discussed in [how anxiety shows up in the body](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/anxiety-in-the-body-not-just-the-mind), except here it is happening between two people at once, each one amplifying the other.

## Why This Isn't About Who Cares More

A common misread in these dynamics is that the pursuer cares more about the relationship because they keep bringing things up, and the distancer cares less because they keep leaving. In my experience this is almost never true. The distancer often cares just as much, but has learned somewhere along the way that conflict is dangerous, exhausting, or pointless, and that stepping back is the safest way to protect the relationship from getting worse. A woman I worked with described growing up in a house where arguments escalated until someone was screaming. As an adult, she goes quiet at the first sign of tension, not because she is indifferent, but because quiet was the only thing that ever made her childhood home feel survivable. Her partner, who grew up in a family that resolved things by talking everything out immediately, reads her silence as rejection. Neither of them is wrong about what silence or pursuit means to them. They are just operating from different rulebooks that were written a long time before they met.

## Breaking the Cycle Without Blaming Either Side

The way out is rarely to convince the distancer to stay in the room longer or to convince the pursuer to bring things up less. Both of those asks ignore the physiology driving the behavior. What tends to work better is naming the cycle itself, out loud, at a calm moment, separate from any specific fight. That might sound like: "I think we do this thing where I push and you pull back, and then I push harder because I'm scared you're pulling away, and you pull back further because you're scared of how intense it's getting. Can we talk about how to handle that pattern, not just tonight's issue?" From there, a few concrete adjustments tend to help. The distancer can offer a specific return time instead of just leaving, such as "I need twenty minutes, and then I want to keep talking," which gives the pursuer's nervous system something to hold onto instead of silence. The pursuer can practice tolerating a pause without following the other person out of the room, which gives the distancer's nervous system room to settle instead of feeling chased. These small adjustments are a version of the [repair attempts](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/repair-attempts-the-small-moments-that-save-relationships) that keep a disagreement from turning into a rupture. Neither move solves the underlying issue on the table, but both interrupt the cycle long enough for an actual conversation to happen later.

## When the Pattern Runs Deeper Than This Relationship

For some couples, naming the cycle and adjusting these small behaviors is enough to change how conflict goes. For others, the pursuing or distancing role is tied to something older, a childhood home where closeness was unpredictable, or where withdrawal was the only available form of safety. In those cases, the pattern shows up in more than one relationship, not just this one, and it tends to resist even genuine effort from both partners. That does not mean the relationship is doomed. It usually means the pattern has roots that go back further than the couple sitting across from me, and that untangling it benefits from more than a communication tweak. Individual therapy alongside couples work can help each partner understand what their role in the cycle is protecting them from, which often does more to change the pattern than any amount of scripted phrasing. If you and your partner keep ending up in the same chase-and-retreat pattern no matter how the conversation starts, that is worth bringing into a session, together or separately. It is a pattern, not a character flaw on either side, and patterns can be understood well enough to change.
