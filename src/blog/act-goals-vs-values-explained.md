Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) gets described a lot of ways — mindfulness-based, values-driven, focused on psychological flexibility. Those descriptions are accurate, but they can also make ACT sound abstract in a way that doesn't explain what actually happens in the room. One of the clearest, most useful pieces of ACT is a distinction most people have never had spelled out: the difference between a **goal** and a **value**.

It sounds like semantics. It isn't. Confusing the two is one of the quieter reasons people work incredibly hard, achieve real things, and still feel like something's off.

## Goals Are Destinations. Values Are Directions.

A goal is something you complete. Get the promotion. Finish the degree. Get married. Pay off the debt. Goals have a finish line, and once you cross it, the goal is done.

A value isn't something you finish — it's a direction you keep choosing to move in. "Being a present partner" isn't a goal you check off. "Being honest, even when it's uncomfortable" isn't something you complete once and never think about again. Values are ongoing. You don't arrive at them; you orient your life around them, over and over, in small moments.

The problem is that most of us were only ever taught to think in goals. School, careers, even a lot of self-improvement culture is built entirely around finish lines. So when someone feels stuck or empty, the instinct is almost always to set another goal — a new job, a new habit, a new milestone — hoping this one will be the one that finally lands.

## Why Hitting the Goal Doesn't Always Fix the Feeling

This is where a lot of high-functioning people get confused. They've hit goal after goal — the career, the house, the relationship status — and they still feel hollow, restless, or quietly disconnected from their own life. It can look a lot like [high-functioning depression](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/high-functioning-depression): successful on paper, empty underneath.

ACT would say the issue often isn't that the goals were wrong. It's that the goals weren't attached to anything underneath them. A promotion pursued in service of a value like "providing security for my family" or "doing meaningful work" feels different than one pursued because it was simply the next rung on a ladder. The achievement is the same. The experience of it isn't.

This is also why insight alone rarely changes behavior. You can *know* your job isn't fulfilling and still not know what to do differently, because knowing the problem isn't the same as knowing your values — [understanding why you do something isn't the same as being able to stop or redirect it](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/why-insight-isnt-enough-to-change).

## Values Aren't Feelings, Either

It's worth being precise here, because values also get confused with emotions. A value isn't "I want to feel confident" or "I want to feel happy." Feelings come and go regardless of what you do — that's just how nervous systems work. A value is something more like "I want to show up prepared, even when I feel anxious" or "I want to stay engaged with my kids, even on days I feel exhausted."

This matters because a lot of coping strategies quietly aim at the wrong target. They're built to control or eliminate a feeling, rather than to support a direction. That's part of why certain coping tools stop working over time — they were never pointed at anything besides feeling different in the moment, which [isn't the same as building something that holds up](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/coping-skills-that-actually-work).

## What This Looks Like in Therapy

In practice, this isn't about writing a values statement and moving on. It's slower than that. It usually involves noticing where someone has been running on autopilot toward goals that used to matter, or were never really theirs to begin with — sometimes shaped by [a fixed mindset absorbed early on](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/growth-mindset) or by what a family or career track rewarded rather than what actually feels meaningful.

From there, the work is about building a life — even in small, unglamorous ways — that lines up with what someone actually cares about, rather than what they think they're supposed to want.

## The Point Isn't to Feel Better. It's to Live Fuller.

ACT doesn't promise that clarifying your values will make anxiety disappear or guarantee a particular outcome. What it offers is a different question to ask, especially when a goal has been met and something still feels off: not "what should I achieve next," but "what am I actually moving toward, and does this still matter to me."

If you've been chasing goals and still feel disconnected from your own life, that's a reasonable thing to bring into a therapy room. Reach out to schedule a session — it's a place to slow down and figure out what you're actually working toward, not just what's next on the list.
