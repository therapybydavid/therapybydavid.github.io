Caregiving is hard enough when the relationship is uncomplicated. You're tired, stretched thin, grieving the slow loss of someone you love. But there's a version of caregiving that doesn't get talked about nearly as much: caring for a parent who wasn't safe, present, or loving when you were the one who needed care.

Maybe your mother was emotionally distant or volatile. Maybe your father drank, or disappeared, or made you feel like your needs were an inconvenience. Now you're the one showing up to appointments, managing medications, answering 2 a.m. phone calls — and somewhere underneath the exhaustion is a question you can't quite say out loud: **Why am I doing this for someone who never did this for me?**

## Two Truths That Don't Cancel Each Other Out

You can feel obligated to help and still feel resentful. You can love your parent and still carry real grief or anger about how they treated you. Neither feeling erases the other. A lot of adult children in this position try to force themselves into one lane — either "I'm a good, dutiful child who doesn't feel bitter" or "I'm so angry I shouldn't feel anything but obligation." Real life is messier than that, and it doesn't mean something is wrong with you.

This kind of caregiving often reopens old wounds. Sitting with a parent who is now frail or confused can bring back memories of being small and unprotected. It's not unusual to find yourself back in old roles — the peacemaker, the invisible one, the one who has to manage everyone's emotions — even decades later. If this pattern feels familiar, it's worth looking at how [childhood emotional neglect shows up in adult relationships](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/childhood-neglect-relationships), because caregiving tends to activate those same dynamics.

## The Guilt Isn't Proof You're Doing Something Wrong

Guilt shows up for a lot of reasons in this situation:

- Guilt for feeling relieved when a parent is hospitalized and you get a break.
- Guilt for setting a limit on how much you'll do or tolerate.
- Guilt for still wanting an apology that may never come.
- Guilt for grieving a relationship that was never actually good, and wondering if you're allowed to.

Guilt in this context often isn't a signal that you're failing your parent. It's often a leftover rule from childhood — that your job was to manage their needs and feelings before your own. That rule doesn't disappear just because the roles have technically reversed.

## You're Allowed to Grieve Something That Never Existed

One of the harder parts of caregiving for a difficult parent is the grief that has no clear shape. You may be grieving the parent they're becoming — weaker, more dependent — while also grieving the parent they never were. There's no funeral for that second loss, no card people send, no script for how to talk about it. If that resonates, [grieving losses that don't fit the usual definition](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/grief-without-a-body-ambiguous-loss) is worth reading, because ambiguous loss often runs alongside caregiving in these situations.

## Boundaries Are Still Allowed

Caring for a parent doesn't obligate you to relive old harm without limits. You can help coordinate care without being the one who absorbs every outburst. You can show up for medical needs without reopening every emotional wound on demand. Boundaries here aren't about punishing a parent — they're about making sure caregiving doesn't cost you your own stability.

This might look like:

- Involving other family members or professional support so you're not the only line of defense.
- Deciding in advance what you will and won't discuss during visits or calls.
- Giving yourself permission to step back when a conversation turns harmful, even mid-caregiving-task.

None of this requires resolving the past first. You don't have to forgive, understand, or make peace with what happened in order to set a limit today.

## You Don't Have to Sort This Out Alone

Caregiving for a parent who hurt you sits at the intersection of grief, obligation, old trauma, and present-day exhaustion. It's a lot to carry, and it's not something you're meant to figure out by yourself in the car after a hard visit.

If this is where you are, therapy can offer a place to untangle the guilt, the grief, and the caregiving logistics without judgment — and to figure out what boundaries actually make sense for you.
