## "I tried meditating. I'm bad at it."

I hear a version of this almost every week. Someone downloaded an app, sat down, lasted four minutes, and got up convinced they had failed. Their mind wandered. They thought about the grocery list, an email they should have sent, whether they were even doing it right.

Here's the part nobody explains up front. A wandering mind isn't a sign that meditation isn't working. It's the raw material. If your mind never wandered, there'd be nothing to practice.

The goal was never a blank head. The goal is noticing that you left, and coming back. That's it. That's the rep.

## What you're actually training

Think about what overthinking does to your attention. A thought shows up, you follow it, and by the time you look up you've been gone twenty minutes, rehearsing a conversation that hasn't happened yet. The problem isn't that you have anxious thoughts. Everybody does. The problem is how long you stay inside one before you notice you're in it.

Meditation shortens that gap. You sit down, you pick something simple to pay attention to, your mind leaves, you notice, you come back. Do that ten times in five minutes and you've practiced the exact skill you need at eleven at night when your brain decides to relitigate your career.

So the wandering isn't the interruption. It's the whole point. A session where your mind wandered forty times is a session with forty reps in it.

## What it does, and what it doesn't

Meditation has a real research base behind it for stress, attention, and emotional regulation. People generally report feeling a little calmer, sleeping a little better, reacting a little less. Those are meaningful changes and they're worth five minutes a day.

What it doesn't do is erase anxiety, resolve grief, or undo what happened to you. It's a skill, not a treatment. If you're carrying something heavy, meditation can make it more bearable to carry while you deal with it. It doesn't deal with it for you. I'd rather say that now than have you quit in three weeks because it didn't fix something it was never built to fix.

## How to actually start

Skip the cushion, the incense, and the perfect corner of the house. This is the whole thing.

**Pick one anchor.** For most people starting out, that's the breath. Not controlling it, just feeling it. The air at your nostrils, your ribs moving, your stomach rising. Whatever's easiest to notice.

**Set a timer for five minutes.** Not twenty. Five minutes you'll actually do beats twenty you'll dread and skip.

**Sit so you're comfortable but awake.** A chair with your feet flat on the floor is fine. Cross-legged on the floor is fine. Lying down usually turns into a nap. Upright without being rigid.

**Count your breaths to ten, then start over.** The counting gives your mind a job. When you notice you're somewhere around forty-seven, or you've lost the count entirely, go back to one. Losing count is normal and doesn't require a comment.

**Stay reasonably still.** You'll get an itch. You'll want to shift. See if you can notice the urge for a few seconds before you act on it. That small pause is the same muscle you use when you want to fire off a text you'll regret.

**When you notice you left, come back without the commentary.** This is the practice. Not "great, I'm bad at this again." Just: oh, thinking. Back to the breath. The self-criticism is optional, and it costs you more than the wandering does.

**Do it at the same time every day.** Attach it to something you already do. Right after you brush your teeth. Right before you start the car in the driveway. Consistency does more work here than duration.

## What the first two weeks feel like

Mostly boring, sometimes irritating, occasionally restless in a way that catches people off guard. All normal.

Some people also notice that when the noise drops, the feelings underneath get louder. You sit down for five quiet minutes and suddenly you're aware of how tired you are, or how angry, or how sad. That isn't the meditation creating something. That's what was already there, without the usual distraction sitting on top of it.

If that happens and it stays manageable, keep going. It usually settles.

## When sitting still makes it worse

This is the part most beginner guides leave out, and it matters.

For some people, especially people carrying trauma, closing your eyes and turning attention inward isn't calming. It's the opposite. The body goes on alert, memories move closer, and five quiet minutes feels unsafe rather than restful.

If that's you, you're not doing it wrong and there's nothing wrong with you. Turning inward without support can be too much before other things are in place. Try it with your eyes open and a soft gaze at the floor a few feet ahead of you. Try a walking version, where the anchor is your feet on the ground instead of your breath. Try something with more sensory input, like cold water on your hands or a slow stretch, and leave the sitting for later.

And if quiet consistently makes things worse instead of better, that's worth talking through with a therapist rather than pushing through on your own.

## The part that matters

You don't need to become a person who meditates. You need to get better at noticing where your attention went and choosing to bring it back. Five minutes a day is enough to start building that.

If your mind wandered the entire time, you didn't fail at it. You practiced.
