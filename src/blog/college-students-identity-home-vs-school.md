Somewhere around week three of freshman year, a lot of students notice something strange: the person they are at school doesn't quite match the person they were at home. Maybe you're more outspoken now, or quieter, or you've started sleeping at 2am and eating cereal for dinner without anyone raising an eyebrow. Then you go home for Thanksgiving and suddenly you're back in your childhood bedroom, being asked why you haven't called more, and some of that new self just... evaporates.

This isn't a character flaw or a sign you're "fake" in one place. It's a normal part of how identity develops when you're building a life away from the people who knew you first.

## Why the Split Happens

For most of your life, your sense of self was built inside a specific set of relationships — parents, siblings, hometown friends, maybe a role you played in your family (the responsible one, the peacemaker, the funny one). Those roles felt like *you* because they were reinforced constantly, every day, by people who expected you to keep playing them.

College interrupts that. You're suddenly surrounded by people who have no idea you were the quiet kid in third grade or that you used to get carsick on every road trip. You get to describe yourself instead of being described. For a lot of students, that's the first real experiment with identity — and it can feel more like *you* than the version everyone at home expects.

This is closely related to a pattern we write about in [A Fixed Mindset Isn't a Personality Flaw. It's a Pattern You Learned.](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/growth-mindset) — a lot of the traits we assume are just "who we are" were actually shaped by the environment we grew up in, not chosen freely. College is often the first environment that lets you test that.

## It's Not Lying — It's Code-Switching

Psychologists sometimes describe this as behavioral code-switching: adjusting tone, vocabulary, humor, even posture depending on who you're with. Most people do it to some degree — you probably don't talk to your boss the way you talk to your roommate. What makes the college version disorienting is the *stakes*. Home represents your history. School represents who you're becoming. When those two don't line up, it can feel less like adapting and more like living a double life.

A few signs this has started to weigh on you:

- You feel a low-grade dread before going home, even if you love your family
- You rehearse how to "act" around your parents so they don't worry or pry
- You feel more like yourself around near-strangers at school than around people who've known you for years
- You come back from breaks feeling like you have to "reboot" into your school self

None of this means something is wrong with you. It usually means your sense of self is still under construction — which, at 19 or 20, it's supposed to be.

## When It Becomes More Than Adjustment

For some students, the split points to something deeper: a family system that only had room for one version of them, or a home environment where certain feelings, opinions, or parts of their personality weren't safe to show. If that resonates, it can be worth exploring further — this is a similar dynamic to what we describe in [Why So Many Houston Adults Feel Lonely in a City of 7 Million](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/houston-isolation-loneliness-therapy), where people feel surrounded by others but disconnected from anyone who sees the whole picture of who they are.

It's also common for students who grew up managing a parent's mood, or being the responsible one in a chaotic household, to find that their school self is the first version of them that gets to just be a person instead of a role. That's not something to feel guilty about. It's information.

## What Can Help

You don't have to pick one version and discard the other. Most people eventually integrate — they keep the confidence or independence they built at school while staying connected to the people at home, without shrinking back into an old role that no longer fits. That process usually takes time, and it helps to have a space that isn't tied to either environment — not your roommate, not your parents — where you can figure out which parts of you are actually you, and which were just adaptations to survive a particular room.

If you're a student in Houston, or attending school elsewhere but home for the summer, and this back-and-forth has started to feel exhausting rather than just awkward, therapy can be a place to sort through it without judgment from either side of your life.
