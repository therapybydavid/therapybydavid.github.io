Most couples don't fall apart because they fight. They fall apart because their attempts to stop fighting keep getting missed.

Relationship researcher John Gottman calls these small bids **repair attempts** — the moments in an argument where one person tries, however clumsily, to lower the temperature. A joke. A sigh. A hand reaching across the table. "Can we start this over?" Sometimes it's not even words — it's a change in tone, a softer face, a pause. Repair attempts are how couples pull back from the edge before things go too far.

The problem isn't that people stop trying to repair. It's that in the heat of an argument, repair attempts are easy to miss, dismiss, or misread as something else entirely.

## What a Repair Attempt Actually Looks Like

Repair attempts rarely look dramatic. They tend to be small and a little awkward, which is part of why they get overlooked. A few common ones:

- "Wait, that came out wrong. Let me try again."
- A tired laugh at the absurdity of the fight itself
- Reaching for your partner's hand mid-argument
- "I'm not trying to fight, I just want you to understand."
- Simply going quiet and taking a breath instead of firing back

None of these resolve the conflict. That's not their job. Their job is to signal: *I still want to be on the same team as you, even right now.*

## Why We Miss Them

By the time a repair attempt shows up, both people are often already flooded — that physiological state where the heart rate spikes, the body braces for threat, and the nervous system stops being able to take in new information calmly. In that state, a joke can land as mockery. A hand on the arm can feel like manipulation. A calm "let's slow down" can sound like being told your feelings don't matter.

This is less about character and more about biology. When someone is flooded, they're not choosing to ignore the olive branch — they genuinely can't see it as one. This is part of why [couples so often find themselves stuck in the same argument](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/couples-same-argument) even when both people want things to get better. The repair attempts are there. They're just not landing.

## The Cost of a Missed Repair

When repair attempts go unnoticed often enough, people eventually stop making them. Not because they've stopped caring, but because it feels pointless — why offer an olive branch that keeps getting swatted away? Over time, this can look like someone "giving up" on a relationship, when what actually happened is quieter: they stopped believing their attempts to reconnect would be received.

This is also where [trust erodes in ways that apologies alone can't fix](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/trust-after-conflict-actions-not-apologies). It's not usually one big betrayal. It's a long accumulation of small moments where someone reached out and no one reached back.

## Getting Better at Noticing (and Sending) Repair Attempts

A few things that tend to help, without requiring either person to become a different person mid-conflict:

**Name it out loud when you're trying to repair.** Instead of a subtle joke you hope lands, try saying directly: "I'm not trying to escalate this, I just want to slow down for a second." It removes the guesswork.

**Build in a pause before responding.** A few seconds between hearing something and reacting to it is often the difference between missing a repair attempt and recognizing one. This is a big part of [what it means to respond instead of just react](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/the-pause-before-you-respond).

**Ask what repair looks like for your specific partner.** Some people repair through humor, others through physical touch, others need distance before they can come back to the conversation at all. What feels like a peace offering to one person can feel dismissive to another.

**Track the pattern, not just the moment.** If you notice you're consistently missing each other's attempts to de-escalate, that's worth naming outside of an argument — when neither of you is flooded and both of you can actually think.

## When It's Hard to Get There Alone

Some couples can work this out with time and attention. Others find that the flooding happens too fast, or that old patterns from earlier relationships make it hard to trust a repair attempt even when it's genuine. That's often where couples or individual therapy can help — not to script the perfect response, but to slow the whole process down enough to actually see each other again.
