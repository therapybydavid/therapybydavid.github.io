Most advice about communication is really advice about prevention — use "I" statements, pick the right time, don't bring it up when you're tired. All of that has value. But it assumes the conversation can be steered perfectly if you just say the right words in the right order.

In real life, conversations misfire. You say something that lands wrong. Your tone comes out sharper than you meant. Someone hears criticism where you meant concern. This happens in strong relationships too — not because the people involved are bad communicators, but because communication is live and unpredictable, and no one gets it exactly right every time.

What separates couples, friends, and coworkers who stay connected from those who slowly drift apart isn't the absence of misfires. It's what happens in the sixty seconds after one.

## What a Misfire Actually Sounds Like

A misfire isn't always a blowup. Often it's smaller and easier to miss:

- You make a joke and the other person goes quiet.
- You ask a direct question and it comes across as an accusation.
- You're trying to be helpful and it sounds like you're taking over.
- You say "I'm fine" in a tone that says the opposite.

In the moment, there's a small window where either person could name what just happened. Most of the time, no one does. The conversation keeps moving, and the misfire gets absorbed instead of addressed — until it shows up again later, bigger and less clear where it came from.

## The Repair Most People Skip

Repair isn't the same as apologizing, and it isn't the same as explaining yourself. It's a specific move: naming that something didn't land the way you intended, before moving on.

**Name what happened, not just what you meant.** "I think that came out harsher than I meant it" is different from "I didn't mean it that way, you're taking it wrong." The first acknowledges impact. The second defends intent and quietly blames the other person for their reaction.

**Give the other person room to still be affected.** A repair attempt that demands instant relief ("okay, we're good now, right?") isn't really repair — it's pressure to move on. Real repair can sound like, "I get that it still stings even though I've said sorry. Take the time you need."

## What Repair Is Not

A few things people confuse with repair, worth naming directly:

- **Over-apologizing.** Repeated, anxious apologies often ask the other person to reassure you, which shifts the emotional labor back onto them.
- **Re-explaining your intent.** Once is clarifying. Three times is defending. If you notice yourself repeating your explanation, that's usually a sign you're trying to be understood more than you're trying to understand.
- **Rushing to fix it.** Some misfires need a beat of quiet before either person is ready to talk about what happened. [The pause before you respond](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/the-pause-before-you-respond) applies here too — repair doesn't have to happen in the same breath as the misfire.

## A Simple Structure for Repair

When you notice a conversation has gone sideways, a repair attempt generally has three parts:

1. **Notice it out loud.** "I think I said that wrong" or "That didn't come out how I wanted."
2. **Ask, don't assume.** "What did that sound like to you?" This tells you what actually needs repairing, instead of guessing.
3. **Adjust, then let it settle.** Say it differently if needed, then stop. Resist the urge to keep talking until you feel resolved — resolution is often slower than the conversation itself.

## When Repair Doesn't Land

Sometimes repair attempts get made and still don't work — the same misfire keeps happening in the same shape, no matter how many times it's addressed in the moment. That usually points to something underneath the specific conversation: an old pattern, an unspoken expectation, or a history that a single repair can't touch. ## You Don't Have to Get It Right the First Time

The goal isn't flawless communication. It's the ability to notice when something didn't land, say so, and stay in the conversation long enough to understand why — without needing to be right, or needing the other person to hurry up and be okay.
