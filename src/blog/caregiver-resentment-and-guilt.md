There's a specific kind of silence that follows a thought like *I can't stand doing this anymore* when "this" is caring for a parent, spouse, or child you love. Most caregivers don't say it out loud. They barely let themselves think it. But the feeling shows up anyway — in the sigh before answering the phone, in the flash of irritation over a spilled cup, in the moment you catch yourself wishing, just for a second, that things were different.

Resentment toward the person you're caring for is one of the least talked-about parts of caregiving, and one of the most common. It doesn't mean you love them less. It means you're human, and you're carrying more than one person is built to carry alone.

## Why Resentment Shows Up in the First Place

Resentment isn't really about the person you're caring for. It's about everything caregiving quietly takes: your time, your sleep, your plans, your identity outside of this role. When needs go unmet long enough, the mind looks for somewhere to put the frustration — and often it lands on the person closest to the loss, even though they didn't cause it.

This is different from not loving someone. You can be deeply devoted to a parent with dementia and still feel a hot flash of anger when they ask the same question for the fifth time. You can love your child and still grieve the life you pictured before their diagnosis. Both things are true at once. That's not a contradiction — it's what caregiving actually feels like from the inside.

## The Guilt Is Often Louder Than the Resentment

For many caregivers, the resentment itself isn't the hardest part — the guilt about having it is. "They didn't choose this. Neither did I, but at least I get to leave the room sometimes." That comparison becomes a script that runs constantly, and it rarely leads anywhere kind. It just adds another layer of exhaustion on top of the exhaustion already there.

This guilt often keeps caregivers from admitting how hard things are — even to themselves — which cuts them off from the very support that might help. If this sounds familiar, it may help to look at [When Taking Care of Everyone Else Leaves Nothing for You](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/houston-caregiver-burnout-therapy), which explores how caregiver burnout builds over time, often long before anyone notices it.

## Grieving Someone Who Is Still Here

Part of what fuels resentment is a loss that doesn't get acknowledged: the relationship you used to have. A parent who no longer recognizes you. A partner who can no longer hold up their end of the marriage the way they once did. This kind of grief has no funeral, no timeline, and no clear permission to mourn — because the person is still alive. If this resonates, [When There's No Funeral: Grieving Losses That Don't Count as Loss](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/grief-without-a-body-ambiguous-loss) speaks directly to this experience.

## What Helps Isn't Pretending You Don't Feel It

Trying to reason your way out of resentment — telling yourself you shouldn't feel it — usually makes it stronger, not weaker. Feelings that get pushed down tend to leak out sideways, in snapped tone or flat detachment, which often creates more guilt than the original feeling did.

What tends to help more is naming the feeling honestly, ideally to someone who won't flinch or try to fix it immediately. Sometimes what a caregiver needs isn't advice — it's simply to be heard without being corrected. The difference between those two things is bigger than it sounds, and it's worth understanding if conversations with family or friends about caregiving keep going sideways — [Venting or Asking for Help?](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/venting-vs-asking-for-help) breaks down why this mismatch happens so often.

## You're Allowed to Need Support, Too

Caregivers often become so fluent in managing someone else's needs that their own get filed away indefinitely. But resentment tends to grow in exactly that gap — the space between what you're giving and what you're getting back, including basic things like rest, acknowledgment, or a place to put your own hard feelings.

None of this makes you a bad caregiver. It makes you someone doing something genuinely difficult, without enough support, for longer than anyone should have to do it alone.

## If You're Carrying This

Therapy isn't about convincing you to feel differently about the person you're caring for. It's a place to say the unsayable things out loud, understand where the resentment is really coming from, and find a way to keep going that doesn't require pretending you're fine.

If caregiving has brought up feelings you haven't felt able to talk about anywhere else, reach out. You don't have to carry this quietly, and you don't have to have it all figured out before you do.
