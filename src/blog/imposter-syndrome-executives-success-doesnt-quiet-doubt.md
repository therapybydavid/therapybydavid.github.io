You would think it gets easier. More experience, more credentials, a bigger title on the door. Logically, the doubt should shrink as the evidence of competence piles up. For a lot of the executives and senior professionals I work with, it works the opposite way. The higher they climb, the louder the voice gets: *you got lucky, you're behind, they're going to figure out you don't actually know what you're doing.* This isn't a confidence problem you can fix with another promotion. It's a pattern, and it has a shape once you know what to look for.

## It's Not Shyness, It's a Moving Target

People picture imposter syndrome as something quiet and self-deprecating, a junior employee who hasn't found their footing yet. At the executive level it looks different. It looks like a VP who has hit every number for six years straight and still rewrites her board deck four times the night before, convinced this is the presentation where everyone realizes she's been guessing the whole time. What makes this version so persistent is that the goalposts move. Land the promotion, and the new fear is that you're not senior enough for the new role. Get named to the leadership team, and now the fear is that everyone else in the room has something you don't. There's no achievement that settles it, because the doubt was never really about the achievement. It's about an internal sense of legitimacy that external proof doesn't touch. The first thing most people try is more preparation. If she just knows the numbers cold, rehearses every possible question, anticipates every objection, the fear should quiet down. It doesn't, because over-preparation is a strategy for managing risk, not for changing how safe she feels in her own competence. The prep buys her through the meeting. It doesn't buy her out of the pattern. **A more useful move**: instead of asking "what do I need to do to feel ready," ask "what would I need to believe about myself to feel ready, and where did I learn not to believe that." That second question is uncomfortable, but it's the one that actually goes somewhere.

## The "If They Only Knew" Loop

A specific thought shows up in almost every conversation I have about this: *if they only knew how much I don't know, they wouldn't have hired me / promoted me / trusted me with this.* It's a private, running commentary that plays underneath client calls, board meetings, one-on-ones with direct reports. Here's a scenario that comes up often. A director gets asked a question in a meeting he doesn't have a confident answer to. Everyone else seems to take it in stride. He spends the next three days replaying it, convinced his hesitation was noticed and logged as evidence against him. Meanwhile, the actual people in that meeting have already moved on to the next fire. The gap between what he thinks happened and what actually happened is enormous, but the loop doesn't care about that gap. It just needs one data point to confirm the story it already believes. What people usually try here is reassurance-seeking, asking a trusted colleague "did that seem okay?" or fishing for compliments after a presentation. It offers relief for about an hour. The loop doesn't run on facts, so facts don't shut it down for long. **A more durable approach** is naming the loop out loud, even just to yourself, the moment it starts: "this is the if-they-only-knew thought again." Separating the thought from the identity underneath it is often where the real work in therapy happens, not just managing the anxious moment but understanding why competence never quite gets registered as evidence in the first place. This is closely related to what we cover in [what high-functioning anxiety actually looks like](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/high-functioning-anxiety), where the outside performance and the inside experience are running on two completely different tracks.

## Where the Pattern Usually Comes From

Imposter syndrome rarely starts in the office. Most often it starts much earlier, in a household where love, attention, or approval were tied to performance. A kid who got noticed for grades, not for who they were. A kid who learned early that being "enough" was conditional and had to be re-earned constantly. That conditioning doesn't disappear when you become an adult with a corner office. It just changes venues. The workplace becomes the new arena where worth has to be proven, over and over, because the underlying belief was never "I am capable," it was "I have to keep performing capability or the good opinion goes away." That distinction matters. One is a stable trait. The other is a job that never ends. We go into this dynamic more in [how childhood emotional neglect shows up in adult relationships](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/childhood-neglect-relationships), and the same wiring often shows up at work, just with a different audience. The common first move is to just work harder, since hard work was the thing that earned approval before. It's a strategy that worked, technically, which is exactly why it's so hard to let go of. The problem is it was never actually solving the underlying belief. It was outrunning it. **A concrete starting point**: try to trace one specific memory of when being "good enough" first felt conditional. Not to blame anyone, but to see the pattern clearly enough to recognize it's a learned rule, not a fact about you.

## Why It Isolates the People Who Have the Most Support

Here's the part that surprises people. The more senior someone gets, the fewer people they usually feel they can be honest with about this. A junior employee can admit uncertainty to a manager. A managing director often feels like there's no one above them to admit it to, and admitting it to the people below them feels like a risk to authority. Think about a founder who built a company from nothing and now leads eighty employees. He can't tell his team he's scared the whole thing could still fail, because they need him to be steady. He can't tell his board, because that's not what they're paying for. He can't tell his spouse every night, because she's heard it for years and he doesn't want to be that person at home too. So it stays inside, unprocessed, doing its work quietly. The usual coping strategy is compartmentalizing it entirely, treating the doubt as a private weather system that has nothing to do with anyone else. That works until decision fatigue sets in, and the constant internal monitoring starts eating into the same mental bandwidth needed for actual leadership decisions. This connects directly to what we describe in [decision fatigue and why the busiest people run out of willpower](https://therapybydavid.com/blog/decision-fatigue-why-busy-people-run-out-of-willpower). Managing a hidden internal narrative all day is exhausting in a way that rarely shows up on a calendar. **A practical takeaway**: find one relationship, ideally outside the reporting structure entirely, where the whole story can be told without managing anyone's perception of you. A therapist, in many cases, is exactly that kind of relationship, precisely because there's no professional consequence to being honest in the room.

## What Actually Helps (and What Doesn't)

Most executives have already tried the obvious fixes: more credentials, executive coaching focused on confidence tactics, books on mindset. Some of that is genuinely useful for building skills. What it usually doesn't touch is the underlying belief system that decides what evidence even counts as proof of competence. This is where the difference between coaching and therapy matters. Coaching is often built to help you perform better within the existing framework. Therapy is where you can actually examine the framework itself, where the belief that your worth is conditional came from, and why no amount of achievement has been able to overwrite it. That's slower work. It's also the kind of work that tends to hold up over time, rather than needing to be re-earned with every new title. If any of this sounds familiar, it doesn't mean something is wrong with your ability. It usually means the internal accounting system was set up a long time ago, under different rules, by someone who didn't yet know what they'd eventually accomplish.
